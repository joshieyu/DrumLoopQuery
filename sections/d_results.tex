\begin{frame}{How we evaluate}
  \slidepoint{Everything in the test is new to the model: new rhythms, new drum kits, new voices.}

  \begin{columns}[T,onlytextwidth]
    \column{0.52\textwidth}
      \small
      \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Held out:} GMD test patterns (893 loops), 2 test kits, 5 AVP test people,
              and our own beatboxes.
        \item \textbf{Answer key:} same pattern ID. Each query has one right loop per test kit,
              among 1,786 clips.
        \item \textbf{Metrics:} mean reciprocal rank (MRR, main), Hit@1/5/10, mAP,
              bootstrap 95\% confidence intervals.
        \item \textbf{Compared with:} chance, CLAP, an onset baseline (no learning), and
              ``transcribe, then match'', with paired tests for differences.
      \end{itemize}
    \column{0.44\textwidth}
      \centering
      \begin{tikzpicture}
        \node[dplain] (q) at (0,0) {Beatbox\\query};
        \node[drow] (r1) at (2.9,0.95) {1.\ loop 412};
        \node[drow] (r2) at (2.9,0.45) {2.\ loop 87};
        \node[drow, fill=UCFGold!60, draw=UCFBlack] (r3) at (2.9,-0.05) {3.\ loop 9 \hfill \textbf{right}};
        \node[drow] (r4) at (2.9,-0.55) {4.\ loop 230};
        \node[dnote] at (2.9,-0.95) {\dots};
        \draw[darr] (q.east) -- (1.35,0.2);
        \node[dnote, font=\small, text=UCFBlack] at (2.9,-1.55) {reciprocal rank $= 1/3$};
        \node[dnote] at (2.9,-2.05) {MRR = average over all queries};
      \end{tikzpicture}
  \end{columns}

  \note{
    Everything in the test is new to the model: new rhythms, new drum kits and new voices.
    The library for the test is 1,786 clips, which is 893 held-out loops played on two
    held-out kits. A query's right answers are the clips with the same rhythm, one per kit,
    so about two right answers hidden among 1,786. We rank the whole library for each query.
    The main score is reciprocal rank: if the right loop comes third, that query scores one
    third, and we average over all queries. Hit@10 is how often a right loop is in the top
    ten. Random guessing scores 0.008. We compare against random chance, CLAP, an onset
    baseline that needs no learning, and a transcribe-then-match baseline, which is the
    classic approach rebuilt. The error bars resample the five test people. To say whether
    two systems really differ, we use paired tests across loops.
  }
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Results so far}
  \slidepoint{Encoders match the best baselines on fake beatboxes, and hold up better on real ones.}

  \centering
  \begin{tabular}{lcccc}
    \toprule
    & \multicolumn{3}{c}{Fake beatbox (test people, test kits)} & Real beatbox (us) \\
    \cmidrule(lr){2-4}\cmidrule(lr){5-5}
    System & MRR (95\% CI) & Hit@1 & Hit@10 & MRR \\
    \midrule
    Random chance & 0.008 & 0.1\% & 1.1\% & 0.008 \\
    CLAP & 0.012 (0.010--0.015) & 0.2\% & 2.1\% & 0.007 \\
    Onset baseline (3 bands) & 0.322 (0.286--0.359) & 22.5\% & 51.7\% & 0.058 \\
    Transcribe, then match & 0.298 (0.238--0.361) & 23.5\% & 44.1\% & 0.077 \\
    Our encoder (R1) & 0.294 (0.284--0.312) & 16.7\% & 56.9\% & 0.167 \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}

  \vspace{0.1cm}
  \raggedright\footnotesize
  \begin{itemize}
    \item 1,786 fake queries, 5 held-out people; our encoder is R1. Baselines are told the
          tempo, encoders see it only through clip length. Intervals are rough (5 people).
    \item Across loops, the onset baseline beats R3 ($p=0.005$), not R1 ($p=0.16$).
    \item \textbf{Real beatboxes:} 14 recordings, 12 rhythms, 2 of us. Right loop in the top 10
          for 7 (R1), 4 (onset), 1 (transcription).
  \end{itemize}

  \note{
    On fake beatboxes from five people the model never heard, our encoder scores about 0.29.
    The right loop is in the top ten for more than half of the queries, against about one
    percent for random guessing. The hand-built baselines do just as well: the onset baseline
    with three frequency bands scores 0.32 and transcribe-then-match scores 0.30. Our paired
    tests say the encoders are tied with transcription, and the only clear difference is that
    the onset baseline beats the log-mel encoder. So the answer to our research question, so
    far, is that learned rhythm embeddings match transcribe-then-match but do not beat it.
    CLAP sits at chance, even the music version: it hears what a sound is, not where the hits
    land. Two caveats: the onset baseline and transcription are told the tempo, while the encoders
    only see it through clip length, and with only five test people these intervals are rough. On our own recordings, 14 takes of 12 rhythms from two of us, the picture changes: the onset
    baseline puts the right loop in the top ten for 4 of 14 and transcription for 1 of 14,
    and CLAP for none. Our R1 encoder does better, with the right loop in the top ten for 7 of 14
    and a score of 0.17 against 0.06 and 0.08 for the baselines; R3 is at 0.05, with 1 of 14.
    Every system is far below its fake-beatbox score, even allowing for how small this set is, and
    14 takes from two people is too few to call a winner. We have not yet tested why the
    drop; real mouths are less regular than our simulated ones, and our laptop microphones differ
    from the studio recordings the models were built on.
  }
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What changes the result}
  \slidepoint{Input choice only matters on real voices; messy queries hurt the hand-built systems most.}

  \begin{columns}[T,onlytextwidth]
    \column{0.48\textwidth}
      \centering\textbf{Front-end}\par\smallskip
      \slidefig[4.0cm]{c_frontends.pdf}\par\smallskip
      {\footnotesize Tied on fake beatboxes ($p=0.78$); on our 14 real takes R1 is ahead.\par}
    \column{0.48\textwidth}
      \centering\textbf{Messier queries}\par\smallskip
      \slidefig[4.0cm]{messier_queries.pdf}\par\smallskip
      {\footnotesize Offsets up to 20 ms did nothing. Jitter hurt the onset baseline, noise hurt
      transcription, the encoders showed no significant change. Voice mode flipped the
      hand-built systems.\par}
  \end{columns}

  \note{
    Left: the two front-ends we compared, same encoder and training. On fake beatboxes, three-band
    onsets and log-mel are tied. On our 14 real takes, onsets score 0.17 and log-mel 0.05,
    which suggests the timbre-light input transfers to real voices better, though 14 takes from
    two people is too few to be sure.
    Right: we binned the fake queries by how messy they were. Timing offsets up to 20
    milliseconds did nothing to any system. Jitter hurt the onset baseline, and noise hurt
    transcription. The encoders showed no significant change from any of the settings. The
    biggest effect was the voice mode, the two ways the AVP people recorded: in personal mode
    the onset baseline scores about 0.10 higher and transcription about 0.15 lower than in
    fixed mode, while the encoders barely move. Part of the transcription gap is its hit
    detector finding fewer hits in personal mode. We do not yet know why the onset baseline
    goes the other way.
  }
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What it gets right and wrong}
  \slidepoint{Usually top ten on fake beatboxes; real ones are much harder, least so for R1.}

  \begin{columns}[T,onlytextwidth]
    \column{0.55\textwidth}
      \footnotesize
      \textbf{Gets right}
      \begin{itemize}
        \item Top 10 for 44--57\% of fake beatboxes with the four best systems (chance: 1.1\%).
        \item Encoders stay steady across timing error, noise, dropped hits and voice mode.
      \end{itemize}
      \textbf{Gets wrong}
      \begin{itemize}
        \item First place only 16--24\% of the time on fake beatboxes.
        \item Real beatboxes are much harder: top 10 for 7 of 14 (R1), 4 (onset), 1 (transcription).
        \item CLAP hears meaning, not rhythm: chance level.
        \item Fake queries are sparse: about 15 of a loop's 34 hits are voiced.
      \end{itemize}
    \column{0.41\textwidth}
      \slidefig[3.6cm]{pattern_types.pdf}\par
      {\scriptsize Swung: 30\%+ of hits on triplet steps (221 of 892 rhythms). Busy: more than the median 34 hits.\par}
  \end{columns}

  \note{
    What works: the four best systems find the right loop in the top ten for 44 to 57
    percent of fake beatboxes, against about one percent by chance, and the encoders hold
    steady as the queries get messier. What does not: the right loop is first only about one
    time in five, CLAP cannot hear rhythm at all, and transcription loses hits, especially
    for personal-mode voices, finding about 75 percent of voiced hits in fixed mode but 67 in
    personal mode. Part of the ceiling is the queries themselves: a fake
    beatboxer voices only about 15 of the 34 hits in a typical loop, so even a perfect
    system sees a sparse version of the rhythm. On our own real recordings the baselines do
    much worse, finding the right loop in the top ten for 4 of 14 and 1 of 14, and R1 does better, 7 of 14. By rhythm type, the encoders and the onset baseline barely
    change between sparse and busy loops, but transcription falls from 0.38 on sparse loops to 0.22 on busy
    ones, since it has to find every hit. Swung loops are not harder for the baselines, and the encoders
    do a little worse on them, 0.26 against 0.29 to 0.31 on straight loops.
  }
\end{frame}

        \footnotesize
        \begin{itemize}
          \item More front-ends, including a pretrained music encoder (MERT)
          \item Full recording sessions: about 50 beats each, with and without a click
          \item Fine-tune on 3 of us, test on the 4th
        \end{itemize}
      \end{block}
    \column{0.32\textwidth}
      \begin{block}{Does a quick enrollment help?}
        \footnotesize
        \begin{itemize}
          \item A new user records a few kicks, snares and hats first
          \item Adapt the encoder with LoRA, $k = 0, 1, 3, 5$ examples
        \end{itemize}
      \end{block}
  \end{columns}

  \vspace{0.25cm}
  {\footnotesize\color{SoftGray}\raggedright
  Also: electronic and lo-fi kits, other tempos, and tempo drift, swing drift and mic EQ in the fake beatboxes.\par}
