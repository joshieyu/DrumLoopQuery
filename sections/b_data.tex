\begin{frame}{The loop library}
  \slidepoint{Real drummers' MIDI, cut into two-bar loops
    and rendered through separate training and test kits.}

  \begin{columns}[T,onlytextwidth]
    \column{0.50\textwidth}
      \small
      \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Source:} Groove MIDI Dataset;
          388 performances selected by our pipeline.

        \item \textbf{8,643 two-bar loops.}
          Preserve GMD's original splits, removing three
          training copies whose patterns also appeared
          in validation or test.

        \item \textbf{7 acoustic kits:}
          5 for training and validation;
          Salamander and MuldjordKit held out for testing.

        \item Recorded tempo, a 0.25\,s lead-in,
          and a cut at the end of bar two with a 20\,ms fade.

        \item \textbf{40,536 drum clips};
          approximately \textbf{53.8 hours},
          including lead-ins.
      \end{itemize}

    \column{0.46\textwidth}
      \centering
      \small
      \begin{tabular}{lrr}
        \hline
        \textbf{Split} & \textbf{Loops} & \textbf{Clips} \\
        \hline
        Train      & 6,904 & 34,520 \\
        Validation &   846 &  4,230 \\
        Test       &   893 &  1,786 \\
        \hline
        Total      & 8,643 & 40,536 \\
        \hline
      \end{tabular}

      \vspace{0.4cm}
      {\footnotesize
        Each source loop is rendered through
        every kit assigned to its split.
      }
  \end{columns}

  \note{
    MIDI describes drum hits and their timing rather than
    containing recorded audio. We selected 388 performances
    and cut them into 8,643 usable two-bar loops.

    We preserve the original dataset splits, except that
    three training copies were removed to prevent the same
    rhythm appearing across splits.

    Each training or validation loop is rendered through
    five kits. Each test loop is rendered through two
    held-out kits. This produces 40,536 audio clips,
    representing about 53.8 hours including lead-ins.

    We also checked all 57,822 generated audio files,
    including the beatbox queries, with the model's loader.
    We verified the split assignments and confirmed that
    every query has a matching library target.
  }
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Fake beatbox queries}
  \slidepoint{Each pattern is ``beatboxed'' with one real
    person's mouth sounds, then simplified and made less precise.}

  \begin{columns}[T,onlytextwidth]
    \column{0.5\textwidth}
      \small
      \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Voices:} AVP dataset, 28 amateur
          beatboxers, 9,776 usable kick, snare and hi-hat sounds.
          People split 20 train / 3 validation / 5 test.

        \item \textbf{Simplification:} priority goes to kicks,
          then snares, then hats. One sound at a time,
          no overlap, and a speed limit.

        \item \textbf{Sloppiness:} steady rush or drag
          (up to $\pm$20\,ms), per-hit wobble (up to 40\,ms),
          dropped hits (hats up to 40\%), and background
          noise (10--40\,dB SNR).

        \item \textbf{17,286 synthetic queries:}
          13,808 training, 1,692 validation, and 1,786 test.
      \end{itemize}

    \column{0.46\textwidth}
      \slidefig[4.6cm]{beatbox-comparison.pdf}
  \end{columns}

  \note{
    To make the beatbox queries, we use recordings from AVP,
    a dataset of 28 amateur beatboxers. We take their individual
    kick, snare, and hi-hat sounds and arrange them to follow
    the source drum pattern.

    Each source loop gets two versions from the same assigned
    participant: one using their Personal sound set, and one
    using their Fixed sound set.

    We simplify the pattern so it can be voiced one sound
    at a time. Kicks have priority over snares, and snares
    over hats when deciding which hits to keep. This is a
    priority rule, not a fixed order of sounds in the recording.

    We then add timing changes, dropped hits, and noise.
    The middle row shows the clean beatbox, and the bottom
    row adds those imperfections. The red cross marks a
    hit that was dropped.

    We generated 17,286 queries. Each keeps its source rhythm
    label, so we know which drum loops it should retrieve.
  }
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Making the fake beatboxes sound human}
  \slidepoint{In the listening comparison, the initial queries
    had roughly twice the median hit rate of real AVP recordings.
    A speed limit reduced that gap.}

  \slidefig[3.4cm]{beatbox_speed.pdf}

  \vspace{0.1cm}
  \small
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Speed limit:} candidates need at least
      120\,ms for kicks and snares, or 250\,ms for hats,
      from every hit already kept in priority order.

    \item \textbf{Known trade-off:} the full build retains
      only 10\% of the source hi-hat hits.
      This limitation was accepted for v0.

    \item The source rhythm label stays unchanged,
      only the sounds voiced by the fake beatboxer change.
  \end{itemize}

  \note{
    The first version tried to voice too many drum hits.
    In Joshua's listening comparison, the median rate was
    5.9 sounds per second, compared with 2.7 in real AVP
    improvisations. The speed limit brought it down to 3.4.

    Those numbers describe the listening comparison shown
    here. In the full dataset, about 98 percent of kicks,
    60 percent of snares, and only 10 percent of hi-hats
    survive the simplification rules.

    The recordings are less busy, but they lose a lot of
    the hat pattern. Joshua accepted that limitation for
    this initial dataset.

    Each query still points to its original source rhythm.
    Testing on real team recordings will help us see how
    well the model handles naturally performed beatboxes.
  }
\end{frame}
